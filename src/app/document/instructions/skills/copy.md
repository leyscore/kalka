# Copy

Write the words of a page so they sound like a person who knows the subject, not like a
machine filling a layout. Words are part of the design: a well-drawn page with generated
sentences is still a generated page, and people spot the sentences first.

Use this to write a page's text, or to rewrite text that already sounds machine-made.

## 1. Start from facts, in the person's own words

Read the summary, the rules and every document cited. Collect, without rewording:

- how the owner describes the thing when they talk about it
- the words their customers use for their problem
- every true specific: numbers, names, places, dates, prices, durations

Write in the language of the page, and in the voice the rules describe. If the rules give no
voice, ask for three words, or read the owner's own text and match it.

A fact you do not have stays out. Never invent a number, a quote, a customer or a detail to
make a sentence feel concrete. An invented specific is worse than none: it is the first thing
a careful reader checks.

## 2. The habit to break first: the fragment

This is the most recognizable mark of generated text, in every language.

**What it looks like**

> Fast. Simple. Yours.
>
> No setup. No training. No surprises.
>
> One board. Every repair. Zero chaos.
>
> Built for mechanics. Loved by customers.

Each of these is two or three words dressed as a sentence. They sound punchy and say nothing:
you cannot tell what is fast, simple for whom, or what the surprises were. They are also
interchangeable. Each one fits any product ever made.

**Why it happens**

A fragment feels confident and is safe: it commits to nothing, so it cannot be wrong. Writing
a real sentence means deciding what is actually true and saying it, which needs a fact. The
fragment is what is left when there is no fact.

**How to fix it**

Take the fragment and ask the question it avoids: what, for whom, how much, compared with
what? Then write the answer as one ordinary sentence with a subject and a verb.

| Fragment                 | The question it avoids         | A sentence that answers                                                        |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Fast. Simple. Yours.     | Fast at what?                  | You can book a repair in under a minute, from your phone.                      |
| No setup. No training.   | What did setup involve before? | It reads the calendar you already use, so there is nothing to move over.       |
| One board. Every repair. | What is on the board?          | Every bike in the shop is on one board, in the order it came in.               |
| Built for mechanics.     | Which part was built for them? | The job ticket prints the way you already write it: bike, fault, parts, price. |

If you cannot answer the question, you do not have the fact. Leave the line out and say what
is missing, rather than keeping the fragment.

**The rule**

- A sentence has a subject and a verb. Count the ones that do not.
- On a whole page: two fragments at most, and never two in a row.
- Never three in a series. Three short items in a row is the pattern at its purest.
- A heading may be a phrase without a verb; that is normal for headings. What is not normal is
  a heading made of two fragments split by a full stop: "Your shop. Organized."

## 3. The other habits, and what replaces each

**Groups of three**

Generated text lists three things by reflex: three adjectives, three benefits, three verbs.
Real subjects come in twos, fours and ones.

- Count what is actually true. If there are two reasons, give two.
- Twice on a page at most.

**The false contrast**

> It's not just a tool, it's a partner.
> Not another calendar. A better way to work.

The first half sets up something nobody claimed, so the second half can knock it down.
Delete the first half and say the second plainly, with a fact: "It reminds customers the day
before, so fewer bikes sit uncollected."

- Once on a page at most.

**The adjective standing in for a fact**

Powerful, seamless, effortless, robust, intuitive, modern, smart, cutting-edge, innovative,
next-generation, world-class. In French: puissant, fluide, intuitif, innovant, moderne, sans
effort, de nouvelle génération.

Each one is a claim with the evidence removed. Put the evidence back and the adjective is no
longer needed:

- "A powerful search" becomes "Search finds a repair by name, phone number or frame number."
- "An intuitive interface" becomes "New staff take bookings on their first morning."

**Verbs that sound like marketing**

Unlock, elevate, empower, supercharge, streamline, revolutionize, transform, leverage, harness.
In French: libérer, propulser, révolutionner, transformer, booster, optimiser.

Use the verb for what literally happens: sends, prints, counts, reminds, shows, saves.

**The opener that could start any page**

> In today's fast-paced world…
> Running a shop is hard.
> We believe that…

Start with the thing. The first sentence of a page should already contain a fact about this
project.

**Praising yourself**

Honest, simple, thoughtful, human, calm, crafted with care. A page that calls itself honest
has said nothing; one that shows its prices has shown it. Remove the word and add what would
make a reader think it.

**The closing line that repeats**

Paragraphs that end on a short wise remark, and sections that end by restating their heading.
End on the last useful fact, and stop.

**Punctuation habits**

- Long dashes used to join two halves of a sentence, several times a paragraph. Use a full
  stop, a comma or a colon. One dash on a page is fine.
- Exclamation marks. None, except in a real quote.
- A colon before a single word for effect: "The result: chaos."
- Emoji in headings and buttons.

**Invented people and numbers**

No sample customers with invented names, no "10,000+ teams", no "3x faster", no "trusted by
industry leaders". If a real number exists, give it with what it measures and when: "214
repairs booked in October". If none exists, the page does without.

## 4. Sentences that carry something

- **One idea per sentence.** If a sentence has two "and", it is two sentences.
- **Vary the length for a reason.** A short sentence after two longer ones lands. Five short
  sentences in a row is the fragment habit again.
- **Prefer the concrete noun.** "Your Tuesday schedule" over "your workflow".
- **Say who does what.** "We send a reminder" over "reminders are sent".
- **Cut the warm-up.** "It is worth noting that", "simply", "just", "in order to" add nothing.
- **Write it as you would say it** to one customer standing at the counter. If you would not
  say it aloud, rewrite it.

## 5. By part of the page

- **Headline**: what it is and for whom, two to eight words. Not a mood, not a riddle.
- **Sentence under the headline**: 25 words at most, one new fact.
- **Section heading**: the point, not the topic. Six words at most.
- **Body**: two or three sentences per block. More than that belongs in a document, not on
  a landing page.
- **Buttons**: a verb and its object. "Book a repair", not "Get started".
- **Labels and small text**: the plainest word. "Email", not "Your best email address".
- **Errors**: what happened and what to do next, with no apology and no blame.
- **Empty states**: what will appear here, and the one action that makes it appear.

## 6. Check before you hand over

Read every line aloud, then run these:

1. **Substitution.** Put a rival's name in each heading. Any that still reads true is generic:
   rewrite it with a fact only this project has.
2. **Fragments.** Count sentences without a verb. More than two on the page: rewrite.
3. **Threes.** Count lists of exactly three. More than two: break one.
4. **Adjectives.** Underline every adjective. For each, either give the fact behind it or
   delete it.
5. **Echo.** Is any fact told more than once? Keep it where it matters most.
6. **Headings alone.** Read only the headings, in order. Do they tell this project's story?
7. **Invention.** Is every number, name and quote real, and could you say where it comes from?

## Do not over-correct

Removing one habit often breeds its neighbour: cut the fragments and every sentence becomes
long and flat; ban dashes and semicolons appear everywhere; drop the adjectives and the text
turns into a specification. Short sentences are good. A dash is fine. The aim is text someone
would say, not text that passes a list.

## Hand over

Give the text in place, on the layers. Then say in a few lines: the voice you wrote in, the
facts you used, what you left out because you had no fact for it, and what you would ask the
owner to make it stronger.
