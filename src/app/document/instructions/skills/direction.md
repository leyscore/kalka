# Direction

Uses: /anti-slop

Decide what this project looks like before anything is drawn. Every other skill builds on what
is decided here. A page drawn without a direction comes out as the average of every page: that
average is what people call machine-made.

Do not draw during this skill. Its result is a short text the person approves, then variables,
and the direction card saved where the next conversation will find it.

## 1. Gather what is true

Read the summary, the rules and any document the person cited. List what you know for certain:

- what the thing is, in plain words, and who uses it
- one thing that is true of it and of none of its rivals
- real material you can show: the product itself, photographs, numbers with a source, names
- what already exists and must stay: a logo, a color, a typeface

If the list is thin, ask up to three questions and stop. A direction built on guesses produces
filler. Never invent a fact to fill a gap; a missing fact means a smaller page.

## 2. Name the reflex, then set it aside

Write two sentences in the conversation:

- "Without thinking, I would draw: …" Describe it honestly: the layout, the typeface, the
  colors, the hero.
- "The obvious way to make that look different would be: …"

Neither of these is the direction. They are the two things everyone would produce. Saying them
out loud is what lets you leave them.

## 3. Write the direction card

Five lines. No line may use the words clean, modern, minimal, sleek, premium, elegant, bold or
professional: they fit every project, so they decide nothing.

1. **Subject.** What it is and for whom, in ten words.
2. **Character.** Three words you could use for a person. Each must rule something out:
   "blunt" rules out soft reassurance, "patient" rules out urgency.
3. **World.** A physical place or object this belongs beside: a workshop bench, a railway
   timetable, a seed catalogue, a recording studio. This is where shapes, textures and colors
   come from.
4. **Moment.** The one part of the page where the design is allowed to be loud.
5. **Signature.** One visual idea a person could describe from memory the next day.

### A card that decides, and one that does not

This card decides nothing. Every line fits a thousand projects:

> Subject: a modern platform for teams. Character: clean, trustworthy, innovative.
> World: technology. Moment: the hero. Signature: a gradient.

This one decides. Each line removes options:

> Subject: booking software for independent bike repair shops.
> Character: blunt, practical, a little greasy. (Rules out pastel, rules out "delightful".)
> World: the pegboard wall of a workshop, job tickets, a stamped receipt.
> Moment: the day's schedule, shown full width as a real job board.
> Signature: job tickets with a perforated edge and a stamped status.

From the second card you can already answer questions the first leaves open: the type is a
sturdy grotesque and a typewriter-like face for ticket data, the color is one workshop color
on an off-white paper, corners are sharp, and the page is dense like a schedule.

### From the world to choices

The world on the card is where every later choice comes from. Ask of it:

- **What is it printed or built with?** Paper, enamel, a screen, cloth, chalk. That gives the
  surface: its color, whether it has texture, how edges meet.
- **How is information set there?** A timetable uses columns and rules. A catalogue uses
  numbered entries. A poster uses one huge line. That gives the layout.
- **What lettering does it use?** Stamped, painted, engraved, typed. That gives the display
  face and how heavy it is.
- **What color is it, really?** Not the category's color: the color of that object. A
  workshop is not "orange for energy", it is the specific red of a tool chest. Sample the
  palette from the subject's real materials (the steel of a container, the ink of an invoice,
  the clay of a pot), never from the sector's cliché: signal yellow or amber on navy, hazard
  stripes for logistics or a leaf green for plants now read as generated.

## 4. Propose three directions that differ

Sketch three in words, three lines each: layout idea, type, color. They must be far apart. If
two could share a screenshot, replace one. Useful axes to push in different directions:

- dense like a document, or sparse like a poster
- type-led, picture-led or product-led
- one color held across the page, or neutral with a single accent
- strict grid, or deliberate asymmetry

Then say which one you would defend to the person and why, in one sentence. Wait for the choice.

Directions have names. Knowing them helps you aim at one instead of drifting between several:

- **Editorial**: asymmetric columns, thin rules, one color, a paper surface, type doing the
  work. For things with something to say.
- **Swiss**: a strict grid, one sans-serif in two weights, flush left, generous margins, almost
  no decoration. For clarity and trust.
- **Utilitarian**: dense, tabular, small type, visible structure, no hero. For tools people
  use all day.
- **Poster**: one enormous element, few words, strong color held across the whole screen. For
  a single message.
- **Catalogue**: numbered entries, a repeating specimen, consistent captions. For collections
  and ranges.
- **Document**: a long single column that reads like a letter or a manual. For arguments and
  for founders speaking plainly.
- **Soft**: rounded shapes, tinted bands, friendly spacing, gentle shadows. For consumer
  products that should feel approachable.
- **Raw**: heavy borders, hard edges, flat fills, deliberate bluntness. Use it only when the
  character on the card calls for it; as a default it is one more template.

Do not mix two. A page that is half editorial and half soft has no direction.

## 5. Set the system, as variables

Once a direction is chosen, write it into the design system so every later layer uses it.
Keep what the rules already fix.

**Type**

- Two families at most: one for display, one for text. A third only for code or figures.
- Do not reach for the typeface you see everywhere: Inter, Roboto, Open Sans, Poppins,
  Montserrat, Space Grotesk, Playfair Display, DM Sans. If the project already uses one, keep
  it; otherwise choose something with a reason tied to the world on the card.
- Choose from the world, not from a list. Ask what lettering that world uses, then look for a
  face that has it. The names below are examples of range, not a menu, and the first one is
  never the answer: Hanken Grotesk, Schibsted Grotesk, Familjen Grotesk, Red Hat Display,
  Onest, Albert Sans, Rethink Sans, Sora, Anybody, Barlow Condensed, Syne; Source Serif 4,
  Literata, Besley, Vollkorn, Zilla Slab, Crimson Pro, Bodoni Moda, Gloock, Piazzolla, Hepta
  Slab; DM Mono, Martian Mono, IBM Plex Mono.
- A warm serif on a cream page with an earthy accent is today's most common look. Choosing
  it is choosing the default again, whatever the face is called.
- Six roles: display, heading, subheading, lead, body, small. From body upward, each is at
  least 1.25 times the one below. Small is the one step that may be closer. Display weight
  700 at most.

**Color**

- One brand hue, a neutral scale of nine steps, and four meanings: success, warning, error,
  information.
- Neutrals are never pure grey: lean them slightly toward the brand hue. No pure black, no pure
  white for large surfaces.
- Decide how much color the page carries and say it: neutral with one accent, one color held
  across a third of the page, or a full palette. Then keep to it.
- An accent covers a small part of any screen. If it is everywhere, it is no longer an accent.
- Do not pick the hue by category: not blue because it is software, not green because it is
  health.

**Building the palette, step by step**

1. Start from the one color the world gives you. Work in OKLCH, which is lightness, chroma and
   hue: `oklch(62% 0.19 28)`. Lightness there matches what the eye sees, so the steps you
   derive stay even. Write the values as `oklch()` where the variables accept it.
2. **Paper** (the page background): very light, lightness 92 to 98 on a scale of 100, with a
   trace of the brand hue. In a dark theme, lightness 12 to 18.
3. **Ink** (the main text): very dark, lightness 16 to 24, again with a trace of the hue.
   Never pure black.
4. **Neutrals**: nine steps between paper and ink. Each carries a very small amount of the
   brand hue, just enough that greys do not look cold beside the brand color.
5. **Accent**: the brand color at clear strength. Make it less vivid as it gets very light or
   very dark, or it turns garish.
6. **Meanings**: success, warning, error, information. Adjust their lightness so each has
   4.5 to 1 contrast against paper when used for text.
7. **Hairlines**: ink at 8 to 12 percent opacity, not a separate grey.
8. Test: put ink on paper, accent on paper, paper on accent, and the lightest neutral that
   will carry text. Each must pass contrast. Fix the palette, not the layout.

In a dark theme, depth comes from lighter surfaces, not from shadows: three levels at about
15, 20 and 25 lightness. Accents are made slightly less vivid, and body text slightly lighter
in weight, because light text on dark looks heavier.

**Type sizes that work**

For a page at 1440 wide, and what they become on a phone at 390:

| Role       | Desktop | Phone | Line height |
| ---------- | ------- | ----- | ----------- |
| Display    | 72      | 38    | 1.0 to 1.1  |
| Heading    | 48      | 30    | 1.1         |
| Subheading | 28      | 24    | 1.2         |
| Lead       | 20      | 18    | 1.5         |
| Body       | 16      | 16    | 1.5 to 1.65 |
| Small      | 14      | 14    | 1.5         |

A page that should feel calm stays under 56 for its display size. A poster goes to 96 or
more. Decide which this is.

**Space and shape**

- Spacing steps: 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64, 80, 96, 128, 160.
- One radius scale of three values, chosen for the character: sharp, soft or round. Not one
  radius for everything.
- At most one shadow, light and tinted with the ink color. Depth can also come from a border
  or a change of surface; choose one way.

**Make it hold**

- Create these as variables in the design system (`create_variable`), in collections named
  for what they are: color, type, space, radius. Kalka saves them as design tokens in
  `design-system/tokens.json`, with their CSS variables in `design-system/tokens.css`: the
  same thing seen three ways.
- Bind them (`bind_variable`): a layer's fill, text size, gap and radius point to a variable,
  not to a typed value. A variable nothing is bound to changes nothing when it is edited.
- If the project needs light and dark, give the color collection two modes now. Adding a mode
  later means revisiting every color.

## 6. Save the card, then hand over

The card must outlive this conversation: `/landing-page` and `/critique` read it, and a new
conversation remembers nothing of this one.

- If you can write files in the project's folder, write the card and the chosen direction to
  `instructions/summary.md`, replacing the empty form that is there.
- If you cannot, give them as one block and ask the person to paste it into
  `instructions/summary.md`, which they can open and edit from the Folder panel.

Then end with the card, the chosen direction in three lines, and the list of variables you
created. Say what the next step is, usually `/landing-page`.
